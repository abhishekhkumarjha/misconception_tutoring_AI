1. Introduction
The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) in education has produced a wave of AI tutoring chatbots capable of
holding open-ended, natural-language dialogues with students (Gupta et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2023). These systems
promise to make one-on-one tutoring — long considered one of the most effective instructional methods but difficult to
scale — available to far more learners.
However, a growing body of work has found that most LLM-based tutors, even strong ones, struggle to move beyond
surface-level correctness checking. Gupta et al. (2025) found that while ChatGPT-family models could solve the large
majority of college-algebra problems correctly, their tutoring dialogues frequently contained instructional errors, and only
a little over half were entirely correct as tutoring interactions rather than just as problem-solving ones. Kumar et al. (2023)
showed that LLM-generated explanations can meaningfully improve learning outcomes relative to bare correct answers
— but this benefit hinges on the quality of the explanation, not just its presence.
Critically, an effective tutor — human or AI — does not simply mark a student's answer wrong. It asks: why is the student
wrong? A student who answers "8" to "5 + 3 × 2" may be making an order-of-operations error; a student who says a
fraction gets smaller when you add a positive number to its denominator may be over-generalizing "bigger denominator,
smaller value" without understanding why. These are different misconceptions requiring different corrective explanations,
even though both produce an "incorrect" label under a binary right/wrong framing.
Scarlatos, Baker, and Lan (2025) took a first step toward addressing this gap, showing that LLMs can be prompted to
identify the knowledge component involved in each turn of a tutoring dialogue and to trace whether a student's
understanding of that component is improving over time (a task they call dialogue knowledge tracing, or dialogueKT).
Their method, LLMKT, significantly outperformed prior knowledge-tracing approaches at predicting whether a student's
next response would be correct. However, their work stops at predicting correctness — it does not attempt to
characterize what kind of error the student is making, nor does it close the loop by generating feedback targeted at that
specific misconception.
This paper extends that line of work. We propose and evaluate [SYSTEM NAME], a tutoring chatbot that:
1. Detects the specific misconception underlying an incorrect student response (not just that it is incorrect),
2. Classifies that misconception against a domain-specific misconception taxonomy, and
3. Generates feedback explicitly targeted at correcting that misconception, rather than generic corrective text.
Our central hypothesis is that misconception-targeted feedback produces better learning outcomes than generic
correctness-based feedback, even when both are generated by the same underlying LLM.

2. Related Work
2.1 LLMs as Math/Subject Tutors
Recent work has evaluated LLMs' capability both as problem-solvers and as interactive tutors. Gupta et al. (2025)
introduced a two-part evaluation methodology — benchmarking LLM problem-solving against an existing intelligent
tutoring system, and having human evaluators act as students receiving tutoring — finding a gap between LLMs' raw
problem-solving accuracy and the quality of their tutoring dialogues. We adapt a similar two-part evaluation approach in
Section 4.
2.2 Knowledge Tracing and Misconception Analysis in
Dialogue
Traditional knowledge tracing (KT) methods model a student's evolving mastery of skills over a sequence of graded
responses, but have historically been applied to structured, multiple-choice-style interaction logs rather than open-ended
dialogue. Scarlatos et al. (2025) extended KT into open-ended tutor-student dialogue, showing that LLMs can be used to
automatically tag dialogue turns with knowledge components and correctness labels, enabling knowledge tracing directly
on dialogue data. Their method (LLMKT) is the closest prior work to ours, and we treat it as our primary comparison
point: where LLMKT traces whether a student's understanding of a skill is improving, [SYSTEM NAME] aims to
characterize what specific misunderstanding is driving an incorrect response, and to act on that diagnosis.
2.3 Feedback Generation for Incorrect Responses
Lin et al. (2024) showed that GPT-4 can be used first to classify whether a (human tutor trainee's) response is
pedagogically correct, and then to rephrase incorrect responses into improved ones that match expert-tutor quality, using
few-shot prompting. While their setting is tutor-training rather than student-tutoring, their two-stage "classify, then rewrite"
pipeline is structurally similar to the detect-then-correct pipeline we propose, and we adapt elements of their approach for
the correction-generation stage of [SYSTEM NAME].
2.4 The Value of Explanation Quality in Learning
Kumar et al. (2023) ran large pre-registered experiments showing that LLM-generated explanations improve learning
outcomes on math problems relative to seeing only the correct answer, with the largest benefit for students who
attempted the problem before seeing the explanation. Notably, they found that even LLM explanations containing
arithmetic errors still produced learning gains better than no explanation at all — suggesting that the framing and
structure of an explanation may matter as much as its raw correctness. This motivates our focus on targeted rather than
merely correct feedback.
2.5 Gap Addressed by This Work
Taken together, prior work establishes that (a) LLM tutoring dialogues often fall short of expert quality despite strong raw
problem-solving ability, (b) LLMs can be used to trace whether a student's skill mastery is improving over a dialogue, and
(c) LLMs can be prompted to rewrite an identified incorrect response into a better one. No prior work we are aware of
combines these into a single pipeline that diagnoses the specific misconception behind an error in real time and
generates feedback explicitly targeted at that misconception, then evaluates whether this produces better learning
outcomes than generic feedback. This is the gap [SYSTEM NAME] addresses.
